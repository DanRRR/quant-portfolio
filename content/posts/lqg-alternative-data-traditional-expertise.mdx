## Seminar details

- **Speaker:** Maxime Bonelli
- **Affiliation:** London Business School
- **Format:** In-person and online
- **Host:** Bloomberg

## Summary

The seminar examined how the arrival of stock-specific **alternative data** changes the performance and investment decisions of active mutual funds.[^1] The analysis uses staggered data coverage—including satellite imagery of retail parking lots—to identify how new information affects investors with different sources of expertise.

The central result is that alternative-data coverage reduces active funds' stock-picking ability in the affected stocks. The decline is strongest for funds whose existing advantage rests on traditional expertise, including industry specialisation and geographic proximity.

## Alternative data and investment skill

Traditional investment expertise can create an edge when useful information is costly to observe or interpret. Alternative data changes that environment by making previously scarce signals available in a more systematic form.

The paper's evidence suggests that this does not simply give every investor an additional signal. It can alter the relative value of established research capabilities:

- coverage reduces stock-picking ability in affected stocks;
- the effect is stronger for traditionally specialised funds;
- those funds reduce their exposure to covered stocks;
- capital is reallocated towards comparable firms without coverage.

The relevant competitive advantage may therefore shift from proximity or domain familiarity towards the infrastructure required to acquire, process and integrate new datasets.

## Ownership effects

As traditional active funds reduce their positions, quantitative hedge funds increase their ownership of firms covered by alternative data. Information technology can consequently affect more than forecast accuracy: it can change shareholder composition and turnover.

This creates a broader chain of effects:

$$
\text{new data coverage}
\longrightarrow
\text{relative investment skill}
\longrightarrow
\text{portfolio reallocation}
\longrightarrow
\text{ownership structure}.
$$

If ownership structure affects monitoring, liquidity or corporate decisions, the release of alternative data may also have real effects beyond the trading strategies that use it.

## My interpretation

The main insight is that data does not merely improve the information set; it can **redistribute who possesses an informational advantage**. A signal that becomes easier to obtain and process may devalue one form of expertise while increasing the value of quantitative infrastructure and scalable analysis.

For systematic investors, this also raises a practical question: how quickly does a novel dataset become commoditised? The value of alternative data should be assessed not only through its standalone predictive content, but also through adoption, crowding, implementation costs and the rate at which competing investors incorporate it.

## Questions left open

- Does the effect vary with the cost, exclusivity or latency of the alternative dataset?
- How quickly does the advantage decay as coverage becomes widespread?
- Do traditional funds recover by incorporating alternative data into their existing expertise?
- Are ownership changes accompanied by measurable effects on liquidity or corporate policy?
- How much of the result reflects improved information versus shifts in investor attention?

[^1]: Bonelli, M., and Foucault, T. (2026). “Does Big Data Devalue Traditional Expertise? Evidence from Active Funds.” HEC Paris Research Paper No. FIN-2023-1492. [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4527672).
