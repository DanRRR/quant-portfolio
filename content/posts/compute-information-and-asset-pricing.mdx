# Summary

The seminar[^1] studies **compute as an economic constraint on information processing**. Investors may observe the same public data but differ in how much useful information they can extract because of differences in computational capacity.

More compute can support richer models, larger information sets, and longer optimisation, but its value depends on whether these gains survive out of sample and translate into net portfolio performance. This creates a trade-off between the **cost of compute** and the **economic value of the information it extracts**.

---

# Core idea

The core parameterisation presented was

$$

L(c) = L_{\infty} + \Bigl(\frac{c}{c_0}\Bigr)^{-\alpha},


$$

where $L_{\infty}$ is the irreducible loss floor for the dataset or model setting,
$c$ is compute, and $c_0$ is a reference compute scale. The exponent $\alpha > 0$
controls the rate of improvement.

The power law implies diminishing returns to compute:

$$
c \uparrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
L(c) \downarrow,
\qquad
L(c) \to L_{\infty}.
$$

Compute was discussed in terms of **PetaFLOP-days**, i.e. floating-point operations aggregated over training time.

## Does scaling transfer to finance?

It is not obvious that ML scaling laws transfer directly to financial data. Financial returns have dependence, non-stationarity, low signal-to-noise ratios, and much smaller effective sample sizes.

Their results show feed-forward neural networks diminishing returns to compute, considered through both
prediction loss (MSE) and economic performance (Sharpe ratio).

This is related to, but distinct from, model complexity: more compute can support larger models, longer training, larger information sets, and more extensive optimisation.

## Double descent and optimisation

Classical Bias-variance suggests a U-shaped relationship between model complexity and test error. **Double descent** allows test error to fall again beyond the interpolation region in sufficiently overparameterised models.

A separate issue is **training risk**: different optimisation runs may converge to different solutions and therefore different out-of-sample performance. This is not the same phenomenon as double descent.

Greater parameterisation may also alter the geometry of the loss landscape.
In their experiment, the 1M-parameter model exhibits a substantially smoother
and flatter local test loss landscape than the 200-parameter model, indicating
lower sensitivity to parameter perturbations.
This suggests that overparameterisation may produce more stable optimisation geometry
despite increasing model complexity. However, larger models also incur greater computational
cost, creating a trade-off between the potential benefits of increased model capacity and the
compute required to achieve them.

## Economic interpretation

The Grossman-Stiglitz (CITE) analogy is:

$$
\text{same public data}
+
\text{different compute}
\Rightarrow
\text{different information-processing ability}.
$$

An investor facing limited compute may rationally use a smaller dataset, simpler information set, or simpler model because additional information is costly to process.

**Note**: Flexible models may also extract nonlinearities and interactions unavailable to simple ridge-regression specifications.

## Asset pricing implication

If compute affects signal quality and trading decisions, it may ultimately affect equilibrium prices. This provides a possible microfoundation for heterogeneous information processing and raises the idea of **market-implied compute**: using market outcomes to indirectly infer the value or extent of computational processing.

The empirical question is therefore not simply whether more compute lowers loss, but whether it produces stable improvements in out-of-sample long-short performance after costs and constraints.

Two investors may observe the same public data $D_t$, but different compute budgets $C$ constrain what they can feasibly learn from it.

Let

$$
\mathcal{F}(C)
=
\{f:\text{ feasible under compute budget }C\}.
$$

Potentially,

$$
C_1<C_2
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\mathcal{F}(C_1)\subseteq\mathcal{F}(C_2).
$$

So the asymmetry is not necessarily **access to information**, but **capacity to process information**.

## Compute and learning

More compute may allow:

- larger models and feature sets;
- nonlinearities and interactions;
- longer optimisation;
- potentially better solutions.

But more compute does **not** imply better out-of-sample performance. Double descent suggests that the relationship between model capacity and generalisation can be non-monotonic.

The relevant question is:

> How much compute extracts economically useful information before additional capacity mainly increases instability or overfitting?

## Portfolio value

Prediction improvements matter only if they survive portfolio construction:

$$
C \longmapsto U\!\left(\Pi^{\mathrm{net}}(C)\right),
$$

where $\Pi^{\mathrm{net}}$ accounts for turnover, transaction costs, risk and constraints.

A useful experiment would separately vary information/features, model capacity, and compute/training budget.

## My interpretation

This motivates an optimal-compute problem:

$$

C^* = \arg\max_C \{V(C)-K(C)\},


$$

where $V(C)$ is the economic value extracted from computation and $K(C)$ its cost.

Define $V(C)$ as the economic value extracted using compute $C$. A rational investor chooses

$$
C^*
=
\arg\max_C \{V(C)-K(C)\},
$$

where $K(C)$ is the cost of computation.

My interpretation is that competition, price impact and finite market capacity may generate diminishing returns:

$$
V'(C)>0,
\qquad
V''(C)<0.
$$

The broader question is whether heterogeneous computational capacity can generate heterogeneous effective information processing even when investors observe the same public data.
