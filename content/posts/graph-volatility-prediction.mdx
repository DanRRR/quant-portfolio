Volatility is easier to forecast than return direction because it clusters through time and propagates across related assets. A graph neural network (GNN) offers a way to encode those cross-sectional relationships when forecasting weekly realised volatility.

## Forecasting target

Let $r_{i,d}$ denote the daily log return of stock $i$ on trading day $d$. For calendar week $t$, the annualised realised-volatility target is

$$
 y_{i,t+1} = \sqrt{252}\,\operatorname{sd}\left(r_{i,d}: d \in t+1\right),
$$

with weeks containing fewer than three observations excluded. The one-week shift is important: features available at week $t$ must only predict volatility realised in week $t+1$.

## Why a graph?

At each week, each stock is a node with a feature vector $x_{i,t}$. The graph $G_t = (V, E_t)$ encodes which stocks are allowed to exchange information. A message-passing layer can be written abstractly as

$$
 h_{i,t}^{(\ell+1)} = \phi\left(W_0^{(\ell)}h_{i,t}^{(\ell)} + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_t(i)} a_{ij,t} W_1^{(\ell)}h_{j,t}^{(\ell)}\right),
$$

where $a_{ij,t}$ is the edge weight, $\mathcal{N}_t(i)$ is the neighbourhood of stock $i$, and $\phi$ is a nonlinear activation. The final node representation produces a volatility forecast $\hat y_{i,t+1}$.

The graph is therefore not just an architectural detail. It states an economic hypothesis about which relationships contain useful information.

## Three definitions of relatedness

A useful comparison considers three graph constructions under the same modelling and evaluation framework:

- **Correlation graph:** connect stocks whose rolling return correlation exceeds a validation-selected threshold.
- **Sector graph:** connect stocks sharing a sector classification.
- **Granger graph:** connect stocks when lagged information from one return or volatility series improves prediction of another, subject to multiple-testing control.

The comparison also includes HAR and LSTM baselines. The HAR benchmark uses volatility at daily, weekly, and monthly horizons:

$$
 y_{i,t+1} = \beta_{0,i} + \beta_{d,i}x_{i,t}^{(d)} + \beta_{w,i}x_{i,t}^{(w)} + \beta_{m,i}x_{i,t}^{(m)} + \varepsilon_{i,t+1}.
$$

This makes the GNN comparison more informative: the question is whether cross-sectional message passing adds value beyond strong temporal persistence.

## How to interpret the result

The useful question is not whether a GNN is automatically better than a time-series model. It is whether the graph encodes relationships that add information beyond volatility persistence, and whether any forecast improvement survives portfolio construction, turnover, and transaction costs.

## What can break the result

- **Graph instability:** correlation and Granger edges depend on estimation windows and thresholds.
- **Sample selection:** a changing universe, missing data, or survivorship bias can make cross-sectional results look stronger than they are.
- **Validation reuse:** graph thresholds, correction choices, and early stopping are selected through validation, so the number of validation decisions matters.
- **Portfolio translation:** forecast accuracy and investment performance are different objectives; turnover, leverage, and transaction costs can reverse the ranking.
